Star Wars is not just the most influential film franchise in Hollywood history. It is the most revealing. George Lucas built his galaxy far, far away on the bones of Joseph Campbell's monomyth, Flash Gordon serials, and Akira Kurosawa's samurai epics. The result was a moral universe so clear that children could navigate it: good and evil, light and dark, a farm boy who saves the galaxy and a father redeemed by love. For forty years, every filmmaker who has touched this franchise has had to answer the same question: do you honor the architecture Lucas built, or do you tear it down and build something else?

The five Star Wars theatrical films VirtueVigil has reviewed answer that question with striking clarity. Two films from the original trilogy sit at the top of this list, their traditional scores validated by decades of cultural endurance. Two Disney-era entries hold traditional ground with real conviction, proving the franchise is not ideologically lost under its current ownership. One film sits at the bottom as a monument to what happens when a filmmaker treats the source material as a problem to be solved rather than a legacy to be extended.

VirtueVigil uses a dual-scoring system that measures the density and intensity of both traditional values content and progressive ideological content independently. These are not quality ratings. A New Hope is a better film than The Last Jedi by almost any metric that matters to audiences, but the scores measure ideology, not craft. Each film on this list links to its full VirtueVigil review with complete category breakdowns, trope audits, and parental guidance assessments. The list runs from most traditional at the top to most woke at the bottom.

Five films. Four decades. One galaxy. This is what the numbers say.

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL

#1. Star Wars: Episode IV -- A New Hope (1977)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 24.2 WOKE: 3.4 MARGIN: +20.8 TRAD

Director: George Lucas | Stars: Mark Hamill, Harrison Ford, Carrie Fisher | Year: 1977

Star Wars did not arrive quietly. On May 25, 1977, George Lucas released a film that redefined what movies could be, and nearly fifty years on, it still holds up as one of the most purely traditional pieces of popular entertainment ever made. The premise is simple on purpose: a farm boy from a desert planet discovers he is connected to an ancient spiritual power, joins a rebellion against a totalitarian empire, and saves the day. It is the hero's journey, Campbell's monomyth made literal. Luke Skywalker is nobody special when we meet him. He is bored and restless, staring at twin suns and dreaming about something bigger. By the time the credits roll, he is the man who blew up the Death Star.

The moral architecture of A New Hope is so straightforward it barely needs explanation. The Empire is evil. The Rebellion is justified. Obi-Wan Kenobi is the wise mentor who sacrifices himself so the hero can continue his journey. Darth Vader kills him without hesitation. Han Solo's arc from selfish mercenary to man who comes back for his friends is the most traditional character trajectory in cinema: individual self-interest transmuted into loyalty to something larger than yourself. The Force is presented as a spiritual reality that demands discipline, patience, and surrender of ego. The 3.4 woke score reflects only Leia's capable wartime role and her resistance to casual dismissal, which is handled with period-appropriate restraint rather than contemporary revisionism. This is the template. Everything that follows owes its existence to this film, and nothing that followed has exceeded its moral clarity.

TRADITIONAL

#2. Star Wars: Episode V -- The Empire Strikes Back (1980)

TRADITIONAL TRAD: 20.3 WOKE: 1.7 MARGIN: +18.6 TRAD

Director: Irvin Kershner | Stars: Mark Hamill, Harrison Ford, Carrie Fisher | Year: 1980

The Empire Strikes Back is the rare sequel that improves on everything the original established. It does this not by escalating spectacle but by deepening everything else: character, theme, moral complexity, and emotional cost. The film opens with the Rebels on the run. The victory of A New Hope bought them nothing but time. Luke goes to Dagobah to train with Yoda. Han, Leia, Chewbacca, and C-3PO flee to Cloud City. Both threads end badly. Luke leaves before his training is complete and loses his hand. Han is betrayed, tortured, and frozen in carbonite. By the time the credits roll, the heroes are scattered and diminished.

Irvin Kershner directed this as a film about what it costs to grow up. Luke's training on Dagobah is not a power fantasy. It is a spiritual discipline that demands humility, patience, and the surrender of arrogance. Yoda's instruction that the dark side is not stronger but "quicker, easier, more seductive" is one of the most concise moral statements in American cinema. The film's lowest point is its most traditional: Han Solo, who began the first film as a self-interested smuggler, is lowered into the carbon-freezing chamber after telling Leia he loves her and hearing her say it back. He chose loyalty over flight. The film answers that choice with frozen silence, and that is the point. Doing the right thing sometimes costs everything. The 1.7 woke score is the lowest in the entire Star Wars catalog, reflecting a film whose only ideological content is the moral education of its hero through suffering and sacrifice.

#3. Star Wars: The Mandalorian and Grogu (2026)

TRADITIONAL TRAD: 17.4 WOKE: 1.4 MARGIN: +16.0 TRAD

Director: Jon Favreau | Stars: Pedro Pascal | Year: 2026

The Mandalorian and Grogu is the first Star Wars theatrical film to come from the television wing of the franchise, and the result is a modest, heartfelt adventure that knows exactly what it is: a story about a father and his son. Jon Favreau, who directed and co-wrote, has spent five years building the Mandalorian universe on Disney+, and this theatrical installment does not try to become something grander. It doubles down on the core relationship that made the series work. Din Djarin, the Mandalorian bounty hunter turned adoptive father, is now working for the New Republic, hunting Imperial remnants with his foundling Grogu. The New Republic assigns him a new mission: rescue Rotta the Hutt from a gladiatorial operation on the moon Shakari. In exchange, Din receives a homestead on Nevarro and official recognition of Grogu as his legal son.

That last detail is the key to the whole film. The Mandalorian and Grogu is not about defeating a superweapon or saving the galaxy. It is about a man building a home for his child. The action set pieces are competent. The starship battles are functional. What makes the film work is the quiet conviction that a father's duty to his son is the highest moral calling available to any man, and that everything else, including the fate of the galaxy, is secondary to getting that right. Favreau understands that the Mandalorian's helmet is not a costume gimmick. It is a symbol of restraint, discipline, and a code of conduct that predates and outlasts any government. The 1.4 woke score is the lowest in the entire catalogue, reflecting a film with essentially zero progressive ideological content. At +16.0 traditional margin, The Mandalorian and Grogu proves that a Disney-era Star Wars film can be built on the values the franchise was founded on.

#4. Rogue One: A Star Wars Story (2016)

TRADITIONAL TRAD: 21.0 WOKE: 7.8 MARGIN: +13.2 TRAD

Director: Gareth Edwards | Stars: Felicity Jones, Diego Luna, Ben Mendelsohn | Year: 2016

Rogue One is the best Disney-era Star Wars film. It is also the most honest one. It ends with every hero dead, and it earns that ending completely. The premise is deceptively simple: the original Star Wars opens with the Rebel Alliance in possession of stolen Death Star plans. Rogue One is the story of how they got them. It is a prequel with a predetermined ending. Everyone who matters dies. The audience knows this going in and the film knows the audience knows it. That shared knowledge creates a specific kind of dread that most blockbusters cannot produce: not "will they survive" but "how will they spend the time they have."

Jyn Erso is the daughter of the Death Star's chief engineer. Her father, Galen, was coerced into building it but secretly designed a flaw into the reactor. Jyn's arc is not about discovering her identity or asserting her independence. It is about honoring her father's sacrifice, continuing his work, and giving her life for a cause larger than herself. Cassian Andor, the Rebel intelligence officer, begins the film as a man who has done terrible things in the name of a just cause, and ends it by refusing to do one more terrible thing, choosing to die with Jyn rather than assassinate her father. Every character in Rogue One gets the same arc: self-interest dissolved into sacrifice. The 7.8 woke score reflects the diverse ensemble casting and general anti-authoritarian framing of the Rebel Alliance, elements that register modestly on the progressive scale. The film's 21.0 traditional score, driven by its relentless focus on sacrificial duty, overpowers those elements decisively. When Chirrut Imwe walks through blaster fire chanting "I am one with the Force and the Force is with me," he is not making a political statement. He is demonstrating what faith looks like when it costs everything, and the film treats him with total seriousness.

WOKE

#5. Star Wars: The Last Jedi (2017)

WOKE TRAD: 9.2 WOKE: 20.6 MARGIN: -11.4 WOKE

Director: Rian Johnson | Stars: Daisy Ridley, John Boyega, Mark Hamill | Year: 2017

The Last Jedi is a technically accomplished, cinematically confident film that is also one of the most ideologically aggressive studio blockbusters ever made. Rian Johnson did not accidentally write a movie that deconstructed Star Wars mythology, humiliated its male heroes, and explicitly argued that the past must be destroyed. He did it on purpose, with full awareness, and he has defended every choice since. The 42 percent audience score on Rotten Tomatoes is the market's honest verdict. The 91 percent critical score is the establishment's ideological endorsement.

Let us go through what actually happens. Poe Dameron, the franchise's most charismatic male hero, opens the film by winning a battle and losing a bombing fleet. This is set up so that female authority figures can spend the rest of the film lecturing him about toxic masculinity and the dangers of hot-headed male leadership. Admiral Holdo, introduced as an antagonist to Poe for no narrative reason other than to humble him, withholds vital information from her own officers, then sacrifices herself in a maneuver the film frames as heroic even though it is exactly the kind of reckless impulse the previous two hours spent condemning. Luke Skywalker has abandoned the galaxy, renounced the Jedi, and spends most of the film refusing to help before dying of Force exhaustion after a projection cameo. The film's central argument, delivered explicitly by its villain, is that the past must die: "Let the past die. Kill it, if you have to." Johnson has confirmed in interviews that this is the film's thesis. For a franchise built on legacy, lineage, and the transmission of wisdom from one generation to the next, it is hard to imagine a more direct repudiation.

The 20.6 woke score reflects dense progressive content across multiple categories: gender politics in the Poe-Holdo dynamic, institutional critique through the Jedi Order's reframing as a failed patriarchal system, and a narrative structure that systematically subverts every traditional expectation the franchise established. The 9.2 traditional score captures the few remaining elements, notably Rey's nascent heroism and the franchise's residual aesthetic of good versus evil. The -11.4 margin makes The Last Jedi the only Star Wars theatrical film to score in WOKE territory, and it clears that threshold decisively. The gap between this film and A New Hope is 32.2 points. That is not an accident. It is the distance between two entirely incompatible visions of what Star Wars is for.

The Big Picture

Five Star Wars theatrical films reviewed. Four in traditional territory. One in woke territory. By the raw numbers, the franchise leans 80 percent traditional in VirtueVigil's database. That statistic is both accurate and misleading.

The original trilogy films George Lucas personally oversaw sit at the top of this list with a combined average margin of +19.7 traditional. The Disney-era films average +5.9 traditional, a number that looks respectable until you remove The Last Jedi's -11.4 from the calculation. Without it, the three Disney-era films (Rogue One, The Mandalorian and Grogu, and The Force Awakens if it were reviewed) would average roughly +14.7 traditional. That is still below the original trilogy's numbers but well within the range of films built on the franchise's foundational values.

The problem is not that Disney cannot make traditional Star Wars films. Rogue One and The Mandalorian and Grogu prove they can. The problem is that The Last Jedi was deliberately positioned as the franchise's flagship artistic statement, released as the centerpiece Episode VIII of the Skywalker Saga with a $200 million budget and a 4,200-screen opening. The ideological outlier was given the loudest microphone in the room. Lucasfilm president Kathleen Kennedy personally selected Rian Johnson and has consistently defended his choices. The 42 percent audience score did not matter to the people who commissioned the film because they were not making it for the audience. They were making it for themselves.

The Mandalorian and Grogu's traditional score is the most significant data point on this list because it suggests the franchise's television wing, where Jon Favreau and Dave Filoni hold creative control, understands the assignment in a way the film division under Kathleen Kennedy did not. Whether that understanding extends to future theatrical releases remains an open question. The Sequel Trilogy's damage to the franchise's relationship with its core audience has not fully healed, and one modestly-scaled film about a father and his son will not erase the memory of a film that told those same fans their attachment to the past was a problem to be cured.
