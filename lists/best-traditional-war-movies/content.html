War films are the American moral imagination made visible. They tell us what we think about sacrifice, about duty, about the kind of men we want to be when the shooting starts and the easy choices run out. Hollywood has been making them for a century, and for most of that century the genre was built on a simple premise: the men who fight for their country are doing something honorable, even when war itself is hell.

That premise has become controversial. The modern war film is more likely to be a prosecution than a tribute. The soldier is recast as damaged goods, the mission as imperial folly, and the country he fights for as the real antagonist. You can see this shift in everything from The Hurt Locker (-12 WOKE) to the 2022 All Quiet on the Western Front (-5 WOKE). The genre that once celebrated the men who saved civilization now prefers to interrogate the civilization they saved.

But not all war films surrendered. The ten films on this list refused the new orthodoxy. Each one tells a story about men in combat where sacrifice is real, duty is non-negotiable, and the country they fight for is worth fighting for. VirtueVigil scored every war film in our database using the full VVWS dual-scoring methodology: severity, authenticity, and centrality of every ideological trope, measured independently for both woke and traditional content. These ten films earned the highest net traditional margins in the genre.

Rankings run from #10 to #1 by net traditional margin. Every entry links to a full VirtueVigil review with complete trope audit, creative team analysis, parental guidance, and scoring breakdown. If a film is on this list, the data put it here.

#10 — 1917 (2019)

TRADITIONAL TRAD: 21.28 WOKE: 2.00 MARGIN: +19 TRAD

Director: Sam Mendes • Stars: George MacKay, Dean-Charles Chapman • Runtime: 119 min

Sam Mendes constructed 1917 as a single continuous shot, and the technical achievement is staggering. But what gives the film its traditional power is the story that format serves. Two young British soldiers are ordered to cross enemy territory to stop a suicidal advance that will kill 1,600 men, including the brother of one of them. The mission is not about winning the war or defeating the enemy. It is about saving specific men who will die for nothing if the message does not arrive in time. That is what the best traditional war stories understand: the unit is the moral object, not the nation or the cause. Schofield does not complete his mission for England. He completes it for the men who will be slaughtered if he fails. The film never moralizes, never editorializes, and never tells you what to think about the Great War. It simply shows you a man doing his duty under conditions that make duty feel like a form of madness. TradScore 21.28, WokeScore 2.00. The lowest raw woke score among all ten films on this list.

#9 — Hacksaw Ridge (2016)

TRADITIONAL TRAD: 21.35 WOKE: 2.40 MARGIN: +19 TRAD

Director: Mel Gibson • Stars: Andrew Garfield, Vince Vaughn, Sam Worthington

Hacksaw Ridge is one of the most purely traditional films Hollywood has produced in the last twenty years. Desmond Doss is not a conscientious objector in the progressive sense, a man opposed to war on pacifist or political grounds. He is a Seventh-day Adventist who believes killing is wrong because God says so. He holds his ground not out of ideology but out of Scripture. When the Army tries to break him, he does not invoke his rights. He invokes his faith. The film treats that faith as something real and dignified rather than as a curiosity to be explained away. And then the battle of Okinawa begins, and Doss spends a night lowering 75 wounded men down a cliff while under fire, praying "Lord, help me get one more" after each one. Andrew Garfield plays Doss with a sincerity that never curdles into sentimentality. The film knows that Doss's story does not need embellishment. It needs only to be told straight. TradScore 21.35, WokeScore 2.40. Mel Gibson directed this nine years after his career was ostensibly over, and he made the best war film of the decade.

#8 — Midway (2019)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 22.82 WOKE: 0.70 MARGIN: +22 TRAD

Director: Roland Emmerich • Stars: Ed Skrein, Patrick Wilson, Woody Harrelson • Runtime: 138 min

The Battle of Midway is one of the most improbable military victories in American history. Four Japanese fleet carriers sunk in a single day. A numerically inferior American force breaking the back of Japanese naval power six months after Pearl Harbor. Roland Emmerich, who built his career destroying the White House in Independence Day, approached Midway with something he rarely gets credit for: genuine reverence. The film traces the battle from the Doolittle Raid through the intelligence war that made victory possible, and it does so without ideological embellishment. The pilots are not deconstructed. The mission is not second-guessed. The film simply presents courageous men doing something nearly impossible under fire. TradScore 22.82, WokeScore 0.70. The lowest raw woke score of any war film in the VirtueVigil database. There is barely enough woke content here to register on the scale. Emmerich made a film that treats the men of Midway as heroes and leaves it at that.

#7 — Dunkirk (2017)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 24.08 WOKE: 1.70 MARGIN: +22 TRAD

Director: Christopher Nolan • Stars: Fionn Whitehead, Tom Hardy, Mark Rylance • Runtime: 106 min

Christopher Nolan stripped war of everything Hollywood has taught audiences to expect: no hero's journey, almost no dialogue, no rousing speeches, no romantic subplot. What remains is survival. The three timelines run at different speeds, land running over one week, sea over one day, and air over one hour, converging on the evacuation at Dunkirk where 400,000 men are trapped between the German army and the English Channel. The genius of the structure is that it makes the rescue feel like an inevitability and a miracle simultaneously. The civilian boats arriving from England, piloted by men too old to fight who crossed the Channel anyway because their country asked, delivers the film's traditional payload without a single line of exposition. You see the boats, and you understand: this is what a nation looks like when it refuses to abandon its soldiers. TradScore 24.08, WokeScore 1.70. Nolan made a war film with no politics and no ideology, just the physical fact of men trying to get home.

#6 — Black Hawk Down (2001)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 24.78 WOKE: 2.80 MARGIN: +22 TRAD

Director: Ridley Scott • Stars: Josh Hartnett, Ewan McGregor, Tom Sizemore • Runtime: 144 min

The Battle of Mogadishu took 18 American lives, wounded 73, and lasted 18 hours. Black Hawk Down puts you inside those 18 hours and does not let you out until they are done. Ridley Scott made this film in 2001, and it was released in early 2002, just months after September 11. The timing gave it a resonance the filmmakers could not have anticipated. But the film itself is not a response to 9/11. It is a meticulous, almost documentary reconstruction of what happened when a mission to capture two Somali warlord lieutenants went catastrophically wrong. What makes the film traditional is not jingoism or flag-waving. It is the way it depicts soldiering as a craft and loyalty as the organizing principle of combat. The Rangers and Delta operators in this film do not fight for abstractions. They fight for each other. When a Black Hawk goes down, the question is never "should we go in?" The question is "how fast can we get there?" TradScore 24.78, WokeScore 2.80. The film refuses to editorialize about American foreign policy, and that refusal is itself the traditional position: these men did their job, and the film honors them for it.

#5 — The Patriot (2000)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 24.64 WOKE: 2.70 MARGIN: +22 TRAD

Director: Roland Emmerich • Stars: Mel Gibson, Heath Ledger, Jason Isaacs

The Patriot is the kind of film Hollywood stopped making around the time it started worrying about how America looked to foreign markets. It is unabashedly pro-family, pro-American, and pro-violent-resistance-to-tyranny. Benjamin Martin is a veteran of the French and Indian War who knows what violence costs and wants no more of it. He has seven children, a farm, and nightmares he cannot outrun. He votes against South Carolina joining the Revolution. Then a British colonel murders his fourteen-year-old son in front of him, and the question of participation is settled. What follows is not a revenge thriller but a war film about a father who fights not for independence but for the specific people under his protection. The film's traditional content lives in the tension between Martin's desire for peace and his recognition that peace purchased with a child's blood is no peace at all. TradScore 24.64, WokeScore 2.70. Emmerich's second entry on this list, and like Midway, it earns its traditional score by refusing to apologize for the men it depicts.

#4 — Lone Survivor (2013)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 25.34 WOKE: 2.45 MARGIN: +23 TRAD

Director: Peter Berg • Stars: Mark Wahlberg, Taylor Kitsch, Ben Foster • Runtime: 121 min

Peter Berg made Lone Survivor because Marcus Luttrell asked him to. That origin matters. This is not a Hollywood producer exploiting military sacrifice for box office returns. It is a filmmaker honoring a promise to a man who lost his entire team on a mountain in Afghanistan in 2005 and has spent the years since trying to make sure they are not forgotten. Operation Red Wings. June 28, 2005. Four Navy SEALs insert into the Kunar Province to surveil a Taliban commander. They are compromised by goat herders. The ethical dilemma, what do you do with civilians who will certainly expose your position if released but who have not committed a crime, is the film's moral center. They release them. Within hours, the team is overrun by Taliban fighters. Three of the four SEALs die. Luttrell survives only because an Afghan villager, honoring the Pashtunwali code of hospitality, protects him from the Taliban until rescue arrives. TradScore 25.34, WokeScore 2.45. The film does not deconstruct these men. It honors them, and in doing so it honors the idea that there are things worth dying for.

#3 — Fury (2014)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 31.08 WOKE: 4.90 MARGIN: +26 TRAD

Director: David Ayer • Stars: Brad Pitt, Shia LaBeouf, Logan Lerman, Michael Pena • Runtime: 134 min

Fury understands something that the anti-war tradition in American filmmaking has always struggled to admit: there are things that make war awful, and there are things about war that reveal what men are made of, and those two truths do not cancel each other out. Set in April 1945, when the war in Europe is effectively over and men are dying for ground that will be surrendered by armistice in three weeks, the film follows a Sherman tank crew commanded by Brad Pitt's Don "Wardaddy" Collier. Into this hardened crew comes a clerk-typist who has never seen combat, played with raw terror by Logan Lerman. The film is about what the crew does to him, what he becomes, and what they all choose to do at a crossroads when the tank tracks break and a battalion of SS infantry is approaching. The climactic stand at the crossroads is one of the most defiantly traditional sequences in any war film of the last twenty years. No moral ambiguity, no deconstruction, just men standing their ground because some things are more important than survival. TradScore 31.08. The highest raw traditional score on this list, and the third-highest among all war films scored by VirtueVigil.

#2 — Saving Private Ryan (1998)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 30.80 WOKE: 2.50 MARGIN: +28 TRAD

Director: Steven Spielberg • Stars: Tom Hanks, Matt Damon, Tom Sizemore, Barry Pepper

The first twenty-five minutes of Saving Private Ryan are not exciting. They are terrifying, exhausting, and morally overwhelming in precisely the way the actual Omaha Beach landing was. Spielberg strips the glory from combat and replaces it with something more honest: chaos, fear, and the specific courage it takes to keep moving forward when everything around you is dying. But the Omaha Beach sequence is not what makes Saving Private Ryan the second-most-traditional war film in the VirtueVigil database. What earns that position is the film that follows: Captain Miller and his squad are ordered to find Private James Ryan, the last surviving of four brothers, and send him home. The mission is not strategic. It is moral. The Army, in the person of General George Marshall, has decided that Mrs. Ryan will not receive a fourth telegram. And Miller's squad, ordinary men who want to go home, are asked to risk their lives for a principle they cannot see or touch. The film's genius is that they do it anyway, not because they understand the principle, but because their duty is to follow orders and their hope is that following orders will earn them the right to go home. TradScore 30.80, WokeScore 2.50. The best American war film ever made, and the data confirms what audiences already knew.

#1 — 13 Hours: The Secret Soldiers of Benghazi (2016)

STRONGLY TRADITIONAL TRAD: 32.34 WOKE: 1.70 MARGIN: +31 TRAD

Director: Michael Bay • Stars: John Krasinski, James Badge Dale, Pablo Schreiber • Runtime: 144 min

The highest net traditional margin of any war film in the VirtueVigil database belongs to a Michael Bay movie. Let that sink in. On September 11, 2012, Islamic militants attacked the US diplomatic mission in Benghazi, Libya. Ambassador J. Christopher Stevens and Foreign Service Information Management Officer Sean Smith were killed in the initial assault. Two CIA contractors, Tyrone Woods and Glen Doherty, died in a subsequent mortar attack on the CIA annex after fighting through the night to protect American personnel. The six-man Global Response Staff team that responded to the attacks did so after being told by their CIA chief to stand down. They went anyway. Every man who fought that night knew something that the diplomats and the intelligence officers and the State Department officials in Washington did not: when Americans are under attack, you go. You do not wait for permission. You do not consult the chain of command. You move toward the sound of the guns. The film is not a political statement about Benghazi. It is a film about the kind of men who run toward danger when everyone else is running away, and about the bond that forms between men who have decided together that some risks are worth taking because some people are worth saving. TradScore 32.34, WokeScore 1.70. The +31 margin is the widest in the genre. Michael Bay, whose reputation rests on explosions and spectacle, made the most traditionally American war film in the database because he stayed out of the way and let the men speak for themselves.

What the Data Tells Us About the War Genre

Seventeen war films in the VirtueVigil database. Fourteen score TRADITIONAL or STRONGLY TRADITIONAL. Only three score WOKE LEAN: Full Metal Jacket, The Hurt Locker, and All Quiet on the Western Front (2022). That is an 82% traditional rate in a genre that, by Hollywood's own standards, should be the most ideologically contested category in cinema.

Why? Because war films resist the progressive project at a structural level. The things that make war films work as stories, sacrifice, duty, loyalty, courage under fire, the willingness to die for something larger than yourself, are inherently traditional values. You cannot make a war film that works as drama and also treats those values as suspect. The films that try, like The Hurt Locker or the recent All Quiet remake, end up as psychological case studies or political arguments rather than war films. They substitute interrogation for experience and critique for commemoration.

The ten films on this list understand something that the modern war film has forgotten: the men who fight deserve to be honored before they are analyzed. That does not mean war films must be propaganda. Saving Private Ryan and Fury are as brutal and unsparing about the horror of combat as any film ever made. They simply refuse to conclude that the horror invalidates the sacrifice. The men on Omaha Beach and in the Sherman tanks crossing Germany knew what they were dying for, and those films have the integrity to treat that knowledge as legitimate rather than naive.

If you want to watch men do their duty under conditions that make duty feel impossible, start at #1 and work your way down. These are the war films Hollywood made before it decided the real enemy was always at home.
