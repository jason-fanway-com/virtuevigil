Comedy is the canary in the coal mine. When Hollywood forgets how to be funny, it is usually because it has decided to be something else first: instructive, corrective, self-congratulatory. The modern studio comedy too often arrives with a thesis before it arrives with a joke. The punchline gets pushed aside so the message can land, and audiences quietly stop laughing. That is why the streaming era, for all its volume, has produced so few comedies people actually quote.

The films on this list are different. They understand that a good joke is an act of generosity. It does not need a moral attached. It does not need to signal which team you are on. It just needs to land. Toy Story does not lecture about loyalty; it makes you feel it through two toys who cannot stand each other until they learn they need each other. Groundhog Day does not preach about redemption; it traps a smug weatherman in a loop until he figures out that service is the only way out. These are comedies that trust their audience to get it.

This list ranks the 20 most traditional comedy films in VirtueVigil's database. "Traditional" means the film earns its laughs through character, craft, and honest human stakes without using the genre as a vehicle for progressive messaging. Every ranking is backed by our VVWS dual-metric scoring system. The margin shown is the gap between traditional score and woke score. The wider that gap in the traditional direction, the purer the comedy. Every entry links to the full VirtueVigil review for readers who want the complete breakdown. If you want to laugh without being lectured, start here.

#11. Groundhog Day (1993)

Traditional Score: 20.6 • Woke Score: 1.7 • Verdict: TRADITIONAL • Margin: +19 TRAD

Genre: Comedy / Fantasy / Romantic Comedy | Platform: Rent or Buy | Director: Harold Ramis | Rating: PG

Groundhog Day is the rare comedy that has become a moral touchstone, and for good reason. What looks like a high-concept gimmick is actually a tight, humane parable about redemption through humility and service. Phil Connors begins as a man who believes his intelligence entitles him to treat everyone else as beneath him, and the loop forces him to confront exactly what that arrogance costs. The film never announces its theme. It simply shows a man becoming decent one small choice at a time, and that is why it has been quoted from pulpits, therapy offices, and philosophy seminars for three decades.

#19. Back to the Future (1985)

Traditional Score: 18.2 • Woke Score: 1.0 • Verdict: TRADITIONAL • Margin: +17 TRAD

Genre: Adventure / Comedy / Sci-Fi | Platform: Theatrical | Director: Robert Zemeckis | Rating: PG

Back to the Future is the most quietly conservative film of the 1980s, and its creators probably never thought about it that way. The premise sounds like science fiction but operates as moral argument. Marty McFly's 1985 family is recognizably broken in a very specific way: his father is a failure, not a villain or an abuser, just a man who never became what he should have been. By going back in time, Marty learns that courage is a choice, that character is built in small moments, and that a family can be repaired. It is a time-travel comedy with a thesis about personal responsibility, and it is about as fun as a lecture has ever been.
