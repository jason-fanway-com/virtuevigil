2024 was the year Hollywood aimed its ideological artillery at the awards stage and watched audiences walk out of the theater. Six films earned our STRONGLY WOKE designation -- the most of any year in VirtueVigil's database of over 700 reviews. Four of those six were Best Picture nominees. The Academy threw roses at Conclave, Anora, The Substance, and Emilia Pérez while ordinary viewers checked their phones, walked out, or stayed home entirely. The disconnect between critical consensus and audience appetite has never been wider, and 2024 is the year the data proved it.

We reviewed hundreds of films and shows released or streaming in 2024. This list ranks the 20 most ideologically aggressive among them, from most woke to least. Every entry links to the full VirtueVigil review with complete scoring methodology. The margin -- traditional score minus woke score on our 100-point dual scale -- reflects how thoroughly progressive ideology dominates the work. A -38.75 margin means the film's woke content overwhelms its traditional content by nearly 39 points. These are not close calls.

What made 2024 distinctive: the prestige lane became the ideology lane. Oscar-bait films pushed harder on progressive messaging than any genre except horror, and horror -- traditionally a refuge for audiences tired of being lectured -- produced four of the top eight entries. Netflix, A24, and Amazon competed to see who could greenlight the most ideologically confrontational project. The year's Best Picture winner (Anora) scored STRONGLY WOKE. Several films on this list were box-office disappointments despite rapturous critical reception. 2024 was the year audiences began voting with their wallets against ideological programming, and the industry has not yet processed what that means. #1: Conclave (2024)

Woke Score: 42.6 • Traditional Score: 3.85 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -38.75

Conclave is the most dangerous kind of progressive film: one that is genuinely excellent. Edward Berger's papal-election thriller is visually sumptuous, masterfully paced, and anchored by a career-best Ralph Fiennes performance. All of which makes its ideological argument more effective, not less. The film's central thesis is that the Catholic Church's traditional hierarchy is irredeemably corrupt and that its only hope lies in embracing progressive reform -- embodied in a cardinal with a hidden intersex identity whose election the film frames as divine intervention. The conservative cardinals are uniformly venal, racist, or power-hungry. The traditional Latin Mass is treated as a symbol of regression. At -38.75, Conclave is the most woke film of 2024 and the third-most woke film in our entire database. The craft is undeniable. So is the agenda.

#2: Heretic (2024)

Woke Score: 41 • Traditional Score: 14 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -27

Heretic is not a horror film that happens to touch on religion. It is a theological assault on Christianity dressed in genre clothing. Hugh Grant plays Mr. Reed, a charming sociopath who traps two Mormon missionaries in his house and subjects them to an extended lecture on why monotheistic faith is a system of control built on stolen deities and historical fabrication. The film's argument is exhaustive and unsubtle: religion is a copy-paste operation across cultures, faith is a prison, and the only honest position is atheism. The missionaries' sincere belief is framed as naivety to be systematically dismantled. That Grant's performance is the best of his career only makes the argument more persuasive. At -27, Heretic is an anti-religious polemic that happens to contain jump scares -- not the other way around.

#3: Anora (2024)

Woke Score: 36 • Traditional Score: 10 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -26

The Academy gave its highest honor to a film that argues sex work is legitimate labor, Russian oligarchs are a joke, and the American legal system exists to protect the wealthy from consequences. Sean Baker's Anora follows a Brighton Beach stripper who impulsively marries the son of a Russian oligarch, and the film frames her transactional choices as morally neutral -- survival in a system rigged against her. The oligarch's family deploys lawyers, fixers, and private security to annul the marriage, and the film's sympathies never waver from Ani's perspective. Mikey Madison is extraordinary in the role, and Baker's vérité style makes the argument feel observational rather than polemical. That is precisely what makes it dangerous. At -26, the Best Picture of 2024 is a progressive class-warfare manifesto delivered with neorealist credibility.

#4: The Substance (2024)

Woke Score: 37 • Traditional Score: 13 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -24

Coralie Fargeat's body-horror sensation uses prosthetic grotesquerie and gallons of fake blood to deliver a feminist critique of beauty standards so blunt it makes Barbie look like a Heritage Foundation white paper. Demi Moore plays an aging fitness celebrity who injects a mysterious substance that spawns a younger, more perfect version of herself (Margaret Qualley), and the film argues with escalating extremity that the male gaze literally destroys women. Every male character is either a leering executive, a grotesque voyeur, or a passive enabler. The third act descends into a thirty-minute bloodbath that literalizes female rage as physical monstrosity. At -24, The Substance earned a Cannes standing ovation and a Best Picture nomination for a film that uses horror as a delivery system for second-wave feminism with a flamethrower.

#5: Emilia Pérez (2024)

Woke Score: 28 • Traditional Score: 6 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -22

Jacques Audiard managed the rare feat of uniting progressive and conservative critics against him while earning 13 Oscar nominations. The film asks the audience to accept that gender transition is morally redemptive in itself -- that the woman Emilia has always been inside absolves the monster Manitas was outside. #6: Immaculate (2024)

Woke Score: 29 • Traditional Score: 8 • Verdict: STRONGLY WOKE • Margin: -21

Sydney Sweeney spent years developing Immaculate as a vehicle for a message she clearly believed in, and the result is a reproductive-rights allegory wearing a nun's habit. Sweeney plays Sister Cecilia, a young American novice at an Italian convent who discovers she is miraculously pregnant despite being a virgin. The miracle is a lie: the convent's male leadership artificially inseminated her with what they believe is a second Christ, using her body as a vessel without her consent. The film's argument is not buried in subtext -- it is the text. A woman's body is taken over by male religious authority against her will, and her only path to freedom is violent self-liberation. At -21, Immaculate is a post-Dobbs horror film that uses Catholic iconography as a stand-in for every institution that claims authority over female reproduction.

#8: Love Lies Bleeding (2024)

#9: Blink Twice (2024)

Channing Tatum plays a tech billionaire who flies beautiful women to his private island where he drugs them, erases their memories, and the film argues this is what all powerful men do when the cameras are off. #10: Babygirl (2024)

Woke Score: 19.22 • Traditional Score: 4.4 • Verdict: WOKE • Margin: -14.82

Halina Reijn's Babygirl is a beautifully shot, expertly performed film built around an argument most traditional viewers will find deeply objectionable: that female submission to a younger male subordinate can be empowering when the woman is in control of her own degradation. Nicole Kidman plays a CEO who begins an affair with her intern (Harris Dickinson), and the film frames her sexual risk-taking not as self-destruction but as liberation from the prison of respectability. #12: Monkey Man (2024)

#14: Nickel Boys (2024)

RaMell Ross made a film that does not want you to watch it -- it wants you to live inside it. The formal innovation is genuine and remarkable. The ideology is baked into the premise: American institutions are built on Black suffering, reform is a lie that masks continued abuse, and the only moral response is witness. At -13.1, Nickel Boys is the most formally daring film on this list, and its argument -- that the American project is fundamentally a prison for Black bodies -- is delivered with the authority of undeniable craft.

#15: A Different Man (2024)

His transformation does not free him; it reveals him. #20: Dune: Part Two (2024)

Woke Score: 19.36 • Traditional Score: 11.55 • Verdict: WOKE LEAN • Margin: -7.81

Denis Villeneuve's second Dune chapter is a cinematic achievement of genuine rarity: a sequel that expands scale, deepens craft, and improves on its predecessor at nearly every technical level. The ideological content is present but subordinate to the storytelling in a way that distinguishes it from nearly everything else on this list. Paul Atreides' arc toward galactic jihad is framed as tragedy, not triumph -- the film is clear that the messianic figure is a manufactured weapon, not a savior. Where it leans woke is in its treatment of the Fremen as noble indigenous resistance fighters whose way of life is morally superior to imperial exploitation, and in Chani's elevation from love interest to conscience -- Zendaya's Chani sees through Paul's manipulation before anyone else and refuses to kneel when the rest of the Fremen accept him as messiah. At -7.81, Dune: Part Two is the most artistically accomplished film on this list and proof that ideological content does not automatically ruin a movie when craft and moral complexity are given equal weight.

The Takeaway

Six STRONGLY WOKE films in a single calendar year is a record. Four of them were Best Picture nominees. That is not a coincidence -- it is a structural feature of how the awards ecosystem rewards ideological conformity. When the Academy's preferential ballot elevates Conclave, Anora, The Substance, and Emilia Pérez while audiences stay home or stream something else, the disconnect is not a bug. It is the system working exactly as designed: critics and industry insiders validating each other's politics while the paying public looks elsewhere.

The horror genre's presence on this list deserves special attention. Four of the top eight entries -- Heretic, The Substance, Immaculate, and MaXXXine -- are horror films. This is not the horror of the 1980s, where teenagers were punished for having sex. This is horror as political argument: religion is a prison, beauty standards are violence, reproductive control is tyranny, and conservative institutions are the real monsters. The genre that used to offer escape from sermonizing has become one of its primary delivery mechanisms. When slasher films and body horror are more ideologically confrontational than the year's political dramas, the landscape has shifted in a way audiences should understand before they buy a ticket.

The box-office data tells its own story. Anora earned $46 million worldwide -- respectable for an arthouse film, but historically low for a Best Picture winner. Megalopolis was a financial catastrophe. Borderlands was a disaster. The Apprentice topped out at $12 million. Audiences are not rejecting these films out of political consciousness; they are rejecting them because ideological messaging has become a reliable signal of a film that prioritizes message over story. The market is not always right, but it is rarely wrong about what people actually want to watch.

What separates the films at the bottom of this list from those at the top is not the absence of progressive ideas but the presence of countervailing ones. Dune: Part Two contains anticolonial politics and a feminist upgrade to Chani, but it also treats Paul's messianic arc as tragedy, respects religious faith as a genuine force rather than a tool of control, and earns its spectacle through craft rather than lecture. Wicked contains a radical political allegory, but it also sells friendship, loyalty, and self-sacrifice with enough emotional conviction that the message goes down like medicine in honey. The films at the top of the list -- Conclave, Heretic, Anora -- offer no such counterweight. They are arguments first and entertainment second.

2024 will be remembered as the year the culture industry learned something it has not yet admitted: audiences can tell when they are being preached to, and they will choose something else when given the option. The films that thrived -- Dune: Part Two, Wicked, Inside Out 2 -- earned their audiences by offering more than politics. The films that failed or underperformed did not fail because audiences rejected their ideology. They failed because ideology replaced craft, and audiences noticed. The lesson is available to any studio willing to learn it. Whether any of them will is a different question.
